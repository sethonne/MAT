Polynomial Interpolation

Newton's Divided Differences Calculator

Interactive learning tool for polynomial interpolation using divided differences.

Definition

Newton's Divided Differences is a recursive method for constructing the unique polynomial of degree ≤ n that passes through n + 1 distinct data points \((x_0, y_0), (x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)\). It expresses the interpolating polynomial in Newton form, where each additional point contributes exactly one new coefficient.

Key Vocabulary

\(x_i\) Nodes — the known \(x\)-coordinates. All nodes must be distinct: \(x_i \neq x_j\) for \(i \neq j\). \(f[x_i]\) Zeroth-order DD — simply the function value \(f(x_i) = y_i\). \(f[x_i,x_j]\) First-order DD — the slope of the secant through two points. \(f[x_i,\ldots,x_k]\) Higher-order DD — generalised divided differences defined recursively.

Recursive Formula

\[f[x_i, \ldots, x_j] = \frac{f[x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_j] - f[x_i, \ldots, x_{j-1}]}{x_j - x_i}, \quad i \neq j\]

The condition \(i \neq j\) (equivalently \(x_j - x_i \neq 0\)) ensures the denominator is never zero, which is guaranteed when all nodes are distinct.

Uniqueness theorem: Given \(n+1\) points with distinct \(x_i\) (\(x_i \neq x_j\) for \(i \neq j\)), there exists exactly one polynomial of degree \(\le n\) interpolating them. Newton's method finds it efficiently in \(O(n^2)\) operations.

Newton Form Formula

The interpolating polynomial written in Newton form uses nested basis polynomials built from the nodes:

\[\begin{aligned} P_n(x) &= f[x_0] \\ &\quad + f[x_0, x_1](x - x_0) \\ &\quad + f[x_0, x_1, x_2](x - x_0)(x - x_1) \\ &\quad + \cdots \\ &\quad + f[x_0, \ldots, x_n]\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(x - x_j) \end{aligned}\]

Compact Form

\[P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f[x_0, \ldots, x_k] \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x - x_j)\]

Coefficient interpretation

Each divided difference \(f[x_0, \ldots, x_k]\) acts as the coefficient of the k-th Newton basis polynomial \(\omega_k(x) = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x - x_j)\). The final polynomial equals \(P_n(x) = \sum_{k} a_k \,\omega_k(x)\) where \(a_k = f[x_0, \ldots, x_k]\).

Applications

Data Curve Fitting.

Interpolating a smooth curve through specific discrete control points.

Estimating Missing Values.

Approximating the value of a function at intermediate points where measurements are unavailable.

Numerical Analysis.

Serving as the mathematical foundation for numerical differentiation and integration (e.g., Newton-Cotes formulas).

Video Game Development.

Interpolation underpins nearly every smooth motion in games. Animation systems use polynomial curves to generate the in-between frames of a character's walk cycle from a handful of keyframe poses. Physics engines fit polynomials to terrain heightmaps so vehicles can follow hills smoothly — exactly what this tool's car demo demonstrates. Camera paths, particle trajectories, and shader colour gradients all rely on the same principle: sample a few control points, recover the polynomial, evaluate it cheaply at every frame.

Spotlight: Video Game Terrain

A 2-D side-scrolling game needs a hill. An artist defines a handful of height samples \((x_0, h_0), (x_1, h_1), \ldots, (x_n, h_n)\) — a few dozen values at most. Newton's method builds the unique polynomial \(P_n(x)\) through those samples; the game then evaluates \(P_n(x)\) thousands of times per second to determine the ground height at any horizontal position.

Mini Example — 3-point terrain

Control points: \((0,\,2),\;(3,\,5),\;(6,\,1)\)

\[f[x_0] = 2, \quad f[x_1] = 5, \quad f[x_2] = 1\]

\[f[x_0,x_1] = \frac{5-2}{3-0} = 1, \quad f[x_1,x_2] = \frac{1-5}{6-3} = -\tfrac{4}{3}\]

\[f[x_0,x_1,x_2] = \frac{-\frac{4}{3}-1}{6-0} = -\frac{7}{18}\]

\[P_2(x) = 2 + 1\cdot x + \left(-\tfrac{7}{18}\right)x(x-3)\]

\[P_2(x) = -\tfrac{7}{18}x^2 + \tfrac{5}{2}x + 2\]

The engine evaluates this at, say, \(x = 1.5\) to get the car's height: \(P_2(1.5) \approx 4.06\) — no artist input needed at that point.

Key Takeaways

Polynomial Interpolation is Fundamental.

Newton's Divided Differences provides an elegant and efficient method to construct interpolating polynomials from discrete data points. Its key strength lies in computational efficiency—adding a new data point requires only \(O(n)\) additional operations rather than recalculating from scratch.

Real-World Problem Solving.

From estimating missing sensor measurements to reconstructing smooth terrain in video games, Newton's method bridges the gap between sparse data and continuous functions. It answers a fundamental question: "What happens between our measured points?"

Gateway to Advanced Concepts.

Understanding divided differences opens doors to numerical differentiation, integration (Newton-Cotes formulas), and even more sophisticated interpolation techniques like spline fitting—all essential tools in computational mathematics.

Practical and Teachable.

Unlike black-box methods, Newton's approach is transparent and debuggable. Each divided difference coefficient has a geometric meaning, making it ideal for educational settings and applications where understanding the "why" matters as much as the result.

Conclusion

Why Newton's Divided Differences? This project was chosen because it represents a perfect intersection of mathematical depth and practical applicability. Newton's method is:

  • Mathematically rigorous: It proves uniqueness of polynomial interpolation and provides \(O(n^2)\) computational complexity.
  • Pedagogically clear: Each step builds intuitively on the previous, making the underlying mathematics transparent.
  • Practically relevant: From engineering simulations to game development, Newton's method solves real problems efficiently.
  • Extensible: It naturally extends to forward differences, backward differences, and more advanced interpolation schemes.

By combining an interactive calculator, step-by-step visualization, and a gamified terrain simulation, this tool brings Newton's Divided Differences to life. Users don't just learn the method—they experience how it transforms sparse data into smooth, continuous functions that drive real applications.

Further Reading & Applications

Real-World Applications

Climate & Weather Modeling

Climate scientists use interpolation to estimate temperature, pressure, and humidity at unmeasured locations. Newton's method efficiently reconstructs continuous atmospheric fields from sparse weather station data.

Medical Imaging & Diagnostics

MRI, CT, and ultrasound machines rely on polynomial interpolation to reconstruct smooth 3D images from discrete scan samples. Newton's method helps deliver pixel-perfect diagnostics in real-time.

Financial Modeling & Risk Analysis

Stock prices, interest rates, and volatility surfaces are often sparse. Traders and risk analysts use interpolation to estimate values at unmeasured price points, guiding billions in investment decisions.

Robotics & Motion Planning

A robot's trajectory is defined by a series of keyframe poses. Newton's interpolation smoothly connects these waypoints, enabling natural, efficient motion without sudden accelerations or jerky movements.

Graphics & Animation (Video Games & VFX)

Every smooth camera pan, character walk cycle, and particle trajectory in modern games and films relies on polynomial interpolation. The terrain simulation in this tool directly mimics how game engines use Newton's method to create immersive, continuous worlds.

Mathematical Extensions

Hermite Interpolation: Extends Newton's method to match both function values and their derivatives, producing smoother curves.

Spline Interpolation: Uses piecewise polynomials to avoid the oscillations of high-degree interpolants, while preserving smoothness.

Chebyshev Polynomials: When you need to minimize interpolation error, Chebyshev points (optimal node locations) can be used with Newton's method.

Numerical Differentiation & Integration: Once you have the Newton polynomial, derivative and integral estimates follow naturally, underpinning methods like Newton-Cotes formulas.
